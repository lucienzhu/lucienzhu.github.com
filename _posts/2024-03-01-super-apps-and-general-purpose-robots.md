Elon’s recent lawsuit against OpenAI has raised an interesting question: does GPT-4 count as AGI? Sam’s remark that AI is a tool rather than a “creature” makes the question even more intriguing.

What form will AGI eventually take?

If we use the relationship to humanity as an axis—whether AI assists people or exists independently of them—we can explore two extremes.

{% include article-image.html src="/img/blog/super-apps-and-general-purpose-robots/tool-and-creature.png" alt="Two directions for AI products: tools and independent creatures" width="1514" height="682" %}

## To the Left: Tools Put People First

Not long after GitHub introduced the copilot concept, OpenAI’s ChatGPT became a sensation. Microsoft responded quickly, elevating it to a product of strategic importance.

From a cloud provider’s perspective, AI could become a universal entry point for user needs. Much like Google Search, it could let cloud companies reach users directly, bypassing individual devices.

A phone extends our senses, putting information in front of us whenever and wherever we need it. An AI product extends the brain, helping us act and decide more quickly and more often. In terms of human augmentation, it fills the missing piece of enhanced cognition. With AI, an individual becomes far more capable at gathering information, processing it, making decisions, and acting on them.

Following that line of thought, a copilot must pursue extreme efficiency. That means:

1. It must be a cross-platform super app, rather than a product tied to one device. It must bring computation, data, and interaction together across different situations, offering a similar experience in different forms on different devices.
2. It will be the tool closest to its user. People will need to share private information with it to receive better, more efficient service. Just as phones gradually changed social behavior, AI tools will change how people think about privacy.

Whoever ultimately wins, the final form will be a personal super app running across operating systems.

A cross-OS experience is not far away. WeChat already spans PCs, phones, and watches; Genshin Impact spans PCs, mobile devices, and consoles. Fundamentally, these products use different front ends—PCs, the web, phones, and headsets—to exchange data with the cloud. When the metaverse concept emerged, Matthew Ball envisioned an experience on the scale of the next internet. AI tools may be what makes it real. The metaverse is not about virtual spaces, but spiritual worlds.

{% include article-image.html src="/img/blog/super-apps-and-general-purpose-robots/apps-engines-and-systems.png" alt="Applications, engines, and operating systems across platforms" width="968" height="762" size="medium" %}

A super app that depends so heavily on private information may seem implausible. But look at what people already ask Google and ChatGPT, and it becomes clear that this is already happening. From the user’s perspective, giving AI access to private information is easy: sometimes there is no need to upload data, only to authorize access through an account. Once a product earns users’ trust, growth may not face much resistance.

One final question: what would it look like? A Silicon Valley startup called brain.ai may offer a glimpse of future interaction. Such an AI tool would generate interfaces and interactions appropriate to the situation, the information, and the device.

{% include article-image.html src="/img/blog/super-apps-and-general-purpose-robots/brain-ai.png" alt="An interface example from brain.ai" width="1200" height="627" size="medium" %}

AI browsers are another interesting product and a sensible step toward a super app. They make good use of internet data to support decisions and can integrate many web services, greatly expanding the range of actions available to users. But as with Chromebooks, computing involves more than the internet. By design, this product does not cover every situation. We will have to keep watching how it develops.

## To the Right: Creatures as Silicon-Based Life

By late 2023, terms such as AI PC and AI browser had become product keywords. Whether as marketing or core functionality, hardware companies and internet products were embracing this shift in what AI makes possible.

- **AI displays.** MSI’s latest monitors offer AI features that recognize and highlight elements on screen, helping League of Legends players understand the situation. A monitor taking on this role can look like it is straying into the territory of cheats. In practice, the much lower barrier to training and deploying AI has brought new kinds of hardware companies into the field.
- **AI phones.** 2024 has been called the opening year of AI phones. Earlier AI features—Face ID, beautification, and HDR—have long since returned to their ordinary names. Today’s generative AI has become the new face of AI. On a device built around frequent interaction, AI also helps compensate for the productivity limitations of a small screen. After market penetration began to level off in 2017, phones have acquired new possibilities.
- **AI PCs.** PCs are joining in too, building on the advantages of their larger form factor for productivity and entertainment. Generative AI is reshaping many creative applications. Running large language models locally improves human-computer interaction: speaking to a computer becomes easier, and assistants become smarter. If operating systems integrate AI further, another wave of upgrades may follow.

Imagine a device with AI that can perceive, decide, communicate with people, and even learn on its own. Doesn’t that look like silicon-based life?

Yet however energetic these efforts are, they remain incremental changes shaped by existing businesses. If AI is a new kind of being, we should eventually see new forms of hardware offering experiences far beyond those of today’s devices.

Starting from first principles and actual use cases, these forms are not hard to imagine. It is more a design problem: what should AI plus mobility look like? What kind of AI would a Mars colony need?

Early robots, such as robotic vacuum cleaners and factory arms, were not necessarily intended to replace people. They were built to solve specific tasks. As perception becomes more general, inputs can largely converge on video and audio. If embodied signals such as touch and spatial perception can also be standardized, general-purpose robots built on general-purpose perception may emerge.

The robots making headlines today fall mainly into two groups. Tesla Bot and Figure use the human body as their starting point; Tesla’s X, Y, and S models use the car. One likely reason is that human and driving scenarios have the richest learning data available. In the future, we may also see dog-like forms, drones, or even Transformers. There is still plenty of room for imagination.

Just as Tesla Bot is intended to go to Mars, an agent’s purpose extends beyond being a chatbot that gives people emotional support. As a creature, an AI being could perceive and interact autonomously, exceed human intellectual and physical abilities, and efficiently convert light into electricity. It may help humanity achieve interstellar exploration, leave Earth, and find its next home.

## One Step Further Is Art

From China’s four early AI leaders to DeepMind, OpenAI, Midjourney, Stability.ai, and the newer AI product companies, an interesting pattern emerges: what we cannot reach remains a distant destination, while what we cannot return to becomes home. Practice always trails the vision.

The first generation called themselves technology companies, but much of their work was labor-intensive data structuring. They focused on data and concrete applications. Guided by an attractive vision, they did the hard work of automation and structuring, while also defining industry standards.

The second generation claimed to be the new entry point for users, but mostly trained models. They pursued an “all you need” approach, learning from the bitter lesson and following the scaling laws of the data age. Under the same ambitious vision, they returned to systems engineering, combining brute force with strong hardware and software.

The third generation is actually building products. What should these companies call themselves?

Their founders have left research labs and large companies, gradually gained control of resources—computation and data—and hope to dominate a new point of entry, as internet companies once did. But by this stage of the technological revolution, the internet already has 5.6 billion users, and products pass the million-user mark every day. Growth is no longer the greatest challenge. Where, then, is the moat?

In user retention and experience design.

To stand out in a crowded market, think different, but keep it simple. That can make an enormous difference.

Perhaps the maturing third generation of AI companies will no longer need to declare what they are. A place name, a phrase, or a symbol may be enough.
